The Board

A computer is a city built on one idea: a switch with no moving parts. This is the motherboard of the example rig — an Intel i7-13700KF, an RTX 4060 Ti, 32 GB of DDR5 — taken apart piece by piece. Tap anything. Follow the power. Then zoom all the way down to a single transistor and flip it yourself. Or skip the tour: build the rig with your own hands, then press the power button and find out if it boots.

New here? The six steps above are the guided path — or just wander. Everything works in any order.

    Explore the board

    Path step 06 · The whole board

    A motherboard is mostly a meeting place: a slab of fiberglass and copper whose job is to connect everything and feed everyone the right voltage. Every part below is real and in roughly its real place. Tap one.

    The whole board

    A meeting place with a power problem to solve

    Strip away the mystery and a motherboard does two jobs. It connects — every part of the machine plugs into it, and its copper traces are the roads between them. And it feeds — it takes the power supply's rough 12 volts and serves every component exactly the voltage it can live on. The example board carries an i7-13700KF, an RTX 4060 Ti and 32 GB of DDR5. Tap any part to take it apart.

    If it diesEverything stops — not because the board thinks, but because nothing can talk or eat without it.

    Follow the power

    Path step 01 · Electricity

    Electricity is just electrons on the move, and voltage is how hard they're pushed. The wall gives your power supply a push of about 120 volts. The CPU would be cooked by anything over roughly 1.3. Somebody has to stand between them — watch who.

    Press run and follow a volt's journey from the wall to the processor. The interesting stop is the third one.

    The zoom ladder

    Path steps 02 · 03 · 05

    Now go down. Board, chip, inside the chip — and at the bottom, the one invention everything else is made of. Each level is roughly a thousand times smaller than the one above it.

    Level 1 — the board

    Thirty centimetres of fiberglass, copper and patience. Everything you've tapped above lives here. On its own the board can't think at all — it's roads and restaurants, not travellers. The thinking happens one level down.

    Level 2 — the CPU

    The processor: a green carrier, a metal hat, and underneath the hat a sliver of silicon smaller than a postage stamp. The example rig's i7-13700KF flips its switches up to 5.4 billion times a second. It draws its ~1.2 volts through the VRM you met above, and it is — all of it — just transistors, arranged with intent.

    Level 3 — inside the chip

    This rainbow sliver is the die — pure silicon, etched with light. Neighbourhoods of transistors form cores that calculate and cache that remembers. The example rig's graphics chip packs 22.9 billion transistors into a die this size. Every single one of them works exactly like the one on the next level.

    Level 4 — one transistor

    A transistor is a tap for electricity with an electric handle. Put a small voltage on the gate and current flows from one side to the other. Take it away and the flow stops dead. No moving parts, nothing to wear out — and it can flip billions of times a second. Press it:

    0output bit

    Gate off → no flow → the output reads 0. That single idea — a switch that electricity itself controls — is the entire computer. Everything else is arrangement.

    Two switches make a decision

    Path step 04 · Logic gates

    One switch is a bit. Two switches, wired with intent, become a decision. Wire them in a row and the lamp needs both — that's AND. Wire them side by side and either one will do — that's OR. Stack enough decisions and you get arithmetic, memory, and eventually a game running at 5.4 GHz.

    AND — both or nothing

    Output: 0 — the lamp is dark.

    In a row, current must survive both switches. A closed, B open? The road ends at B. This is the shape of every "if both are true" a computer ever asks.

    In a real chip these two switches are two transistors, and the "lamp" is the input to the next gate. Chain a few billion and the decisions start to look like thought.

    That's the whole machine

    Electricity, a switch it can flip itself, decisions built from switches, and a board that feeds and connects them — nothing else is in the box. The example rig on Dom's desk is this page, running 5.4 billion flips a second. Fall down seven. Stand up eight.
