Two ships. Which one is the real Ship of Theseus?

The ship in the harbor has never stopped sailing, but none of its wood is original. The ship in the shipyard is built from every original plank, in the original order.

The paradox

The story comes from Plutarch, a Greek writer of the first century AD. In his Life of Theseus, he says the Athenians kept the ship that the hero Theseus sailed home from Crete. As its old timbers rotted, they pulled them out and put in new ones, and they kept doing it for centuries.

The ship became a standing example for philosophers of the question of growth: some said it stayed the same ship, others said it did not.Paraphrasing Plutarch, Life of Theseus 23

So the question is simple. If every part of something gets replaced, one at a time, is it still the same thing? And if it isn't, which plank was the one that made the difference?

The twist: the rebuilt ship

In 1655 the English philosopher Thomas Hobbes made it worse. Suppose someone saved every plank as it came off, and once they had them all, reassembled them into a ship. Now there are two ships. One has the history, since it never stopped being used. The other has the wood, every original piece in its original place. They can't both be the one ship Theseus sailed, but each has a strong claim.

Philosophers sometimes call these two ships Replacement and Reassembly. The instrument above ends exactly there.

How people answer it

There's no agreed answer, but the main positions line up with the buttons above.

The ship in the harbor

What matters is continuity. The ship changed gradually and kept doing its job the whole time, the way a river is the same river even though the water is always new.

The rebuilt ship

What matters is the material. A thing is its parts, so the ship made of the original planks is the original ship, just taken apart and put back together.

Both

Some philosophers say "same" has two senses. The harbor ship is the same ship, and the rebuilt one is the same wood. The puzzle comes from mixing the two.

Neither

Others say "the same ship" is a label people agree on, not a fact in the world. Once a ship is changed enough, there's no hidden right answer, only a choice about what to call it.

When did you stop being you?

The ship is also a story about people. Your body keeps replacing its materials. Skin, blood and the lining of your gut turn over constantly, though not everything does: most of the neurons in your brain are with you for life. Your memories, habits and beliefs change too.

So the same question applies to you. If you're the same person you were at seven, is that because of your body, your memories, the unbroken story connecting then and now, or simply because everyone, including you, keeps calling you by the same name?
