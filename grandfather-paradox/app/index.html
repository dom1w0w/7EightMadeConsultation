The paradox

Suppose you travel back in time and stop your grandfather from ever meeting your grandmother. Then your parent is never born — and neither are you. But if you were never born, nobody traveled back. So he meets her after all, your parent is born, you are born, and you go back and stop him. Both stories can't be true. Each one causes the other.

The puzzle took its classic shape in René Barjavel's 1943 novel Le Voyageur imprudent, where a time traveler meddles with his own ancestry. Physicists and philosophers have been arguing about the exits ever since. The app just makes it personal: not a stranger in a textbook, but your Lolo, your bahay, your photo.

Three ways out

Nobody has settled this. The app lets you play all three classic escapes and feel which one convinces you.

The fixed timeline

The physicist Igor Novikov argued the past can only happen one way: if you go back, you were always part of it. Try to stop the meeting and something — a passing kalesa, the church bells, your own hand passing through his arm — stops you instead. Probability itself conspires to keep history consistent.

Branching worlds

In the many-worlds picture, changing the past doesn't rewrite your world — it splits off a new one. You really can stop him, and a family photo really does empty out. Just not yours. The you who went back came from a world where he walked into that store.

The paradox stands

Or there is no exit: the loop is the answer. You exist because you went back; you went back because you exist. Some stories — and some readings of physics — leave the contradiction standing and let it hum.
