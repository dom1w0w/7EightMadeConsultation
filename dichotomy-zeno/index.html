The gap does not close.

Halve the space between your thumb and index finger. Then halve what's left. Then do it again, forever. This is Zeno's dichotomy paradox, and Dichotomy Zeno lets you feel it with your own fingers.

The paradox

Zeno of Elea was a Greek philosopher who lived around 490 to 430 BC. He's known for a set of short arguments that motion is impossible. The dichotomy is the simplest, and the name just means "cutting in two."

To walk to a door, you first have to get halfway there. Before you finish, you have to cover half of the remaining half, then half of what's left after that, and so on. Since there's always another half to cover, the trip is made of infinitely many steps. Zeno's question is how anyone finishes infinitely many steps in a finite amount of time.

"That which is in locomotion must arrive at the half-way stage before it arrives at the goal."Aristotle's summary of Zeno, Physics VI.9 (239b)

Most of what we know about Zeno comes through Aristotle, who repeated the argument mainly so he could answer it.

Two ways to cut it

The argument works in both directions, and the second one is stranger.

Forward: you can't finish

Half, then a quarter, then an eighth, and so on. Each step leaves a smaller gap, but never no gap.

Backward: you can't start

Before you reach the halfway point you have to reach the quarter, and before that the eighth. There's no first step to take.

The math answer

Add up the steps: ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + 1/16 + … The total never goes past 1, and it gets as close to 1 as you like. Mathematicians say the series converges to 1. Infinitely many pieces can add up to a finite length.

If each step also takes half as long as the one before, the time adds up the same way and comes out finite. That's why you reach the door. This idea of a limit was made rigorous in the 1800s by mathematicians such as Cauchy and Weierstrass.

The running total climbs toward 1 and never passes it.

Why people still argue about it

The math shows the sum is finite. Some philosophers say that doesn't fully explain how a runner actually completes infinitely many separate acts.

Aristotle

A line can be divided forever in principle, he said, but the runner never actually carries out infinitely many divisions. The infinity is only potential.

Supertasks

In 1954 James Thomson imagined a lamp switched on and off infinitely many times in two minutes and asked whether it ends up on or off. Puzzles like this are called supertasks.

Physics

Down around the Planck length, we don't know whether space can still be halved at all. Zeno's question turns into an open question about how nature is built.

Your fingers

On a screen, the gap is just numbers, and the app can keep halving long after your eyes stop seeing a difference. That's the feeling Zeno was pointing at.

Feel it with your fingers

Dichotomy Zeno is free and runs in your browser. Pinch, slide, or halve the gap exactly in two. At the floor of each world it opens outward to the next one: outline, print, cell, atom. It's in English and Japanese.
