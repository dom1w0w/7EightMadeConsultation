Holding the watch: the old stranger

    Who made the watch?

    Four answers get offered for this paradox. Every one of them is satisfying for about five seconds. Pick yours — then watch it break.

    Why every answer breaks

    Your answer
    “You did.”

    Breaks here: you never made anything. You received the watch and you delivered it. At no point in your life did you cast brass, cut a gear, or assemble a movement. Carrying is not creating.

    Your answer
    “Your older self.”

    Breaks here: he didn't make it either. He received it from a stranger when he was young — a stranger who turned out to be you. Pointing at him just moves the question one lap around the loop.

    Your answer
    “No one made it.”

    Breaks here: the watch is real. Brass, glass, a ticking movement — it exists, it works, it can be dropped and dented. “No one made it” means a finished machine can exist with no cause at all. Everything else you own got here some other way.

    Your answer
    “The loop itself.”

    Breaks here: a loop is a shape, not a craftsman. At no point on the circle does anyone perform the act of making — the watch is simply already there, at every point, forever. The circle explains the path. It never explains the thing traveling it.

    It ends the same way. That's the point.

    The idea

    The paradox

    The name comes from Robert A. Heinlein's 1941 story “By His Bootstraps” — after the old impossible saying about lifting yourself off the ground by pulling up on your own bootstraps. Philosophers also call it the ontological paradox, or a causal loop: an object, a person, or a piece of information that exists inside a closed circle of cause and effect, so it has effects everywhere and an origin nowhere.

    The watch is the gentle version. The famous one is music: a time traveler takes the scores of Beethoven's symphonies back to Beethoven, who copies them out and publishes them as his own. The scores exist. The music exists. Who composed it? Not the traveler — he only carried it. Not Beethoven — he only copied it. The symphonies were never written by anyone.

    Hollywood runs on this loop. In The Terminator (1984), the wreckage of a machine sent back to kill Sarah Connor is recovered and reverse-engineered — and the chip inside it becomes the seed of Skynet, the system that builds the machine. Skynet is its own ancestor. And in Somewhere in Time (1980), the whole romance turns on a pocket watch an old woman presses into a young man's hand — a watch he will one day press into hers. It is never bought, never made. It just goes around, aging with the loop, forever.

    The positions

    How people answer it

    “You did.”

    The traveler-centric answer. Without your choices — taking the watch, boarding the machine, making the handover — nothing circulates. On this view, “made” just means “is responsible for,” and the only agent anywhere in the story is you. The loop has no craftsman, but it has an author, and it's the person inside it.

    “Your older self.”

    The deferral. Every hand in the chain points at the hand before it, and the oldest hand in the story belongs to the stranger — so the origin must be his, offstage, before the curtain rose. It feels like an answer because it pushes the mystery out of sight. It works right up until you remember his biography is identical to yours.

    “No one made it.”

    The physicist's shrug, and maybe the honest one. Some solutions to Einstein's equations allow closed timelike curves — paths through spacetime that return to their own past. An object on such a path doesn't need a creation event; it needs only to be consistent at every point. It was never made. It was also never not there.

    “The loop itself.”

    The geometric answer. “Where did it come from?” assumes causes run in a straight line with a first link — true of chains, false of circles. On a circle, every point is explained by the point before it, and the whole figure explains itself. The demand for an origin isn't unanswered; it's the wrong shape of question.

    The trail

    A brief history of the loop

    1941

    “By His Bootstraps”

    Robert A. Heinlein publishes the story in Astounding Science Fiction. His traveler keeps meeting older and younger versions of himself — and the paradox gets its name.

    1980

    Somewhere in Time

    An elderly woman presses a pocket watch into Christopher Reeve's hand: “Come back to me.” The watch he later gives her younger self is the same one. It was never made.

    1984

    The Terminator

    Cyberdyne recovers a chip from a crushed Terminator and builds Skynet from it. The machines end up engineering their own creator.

    1980s

    The Novikov principle

    Physicist Igor Novikov proposes the self-consistency principle: loops may exist, but only ones that never contradict themselves. The watch can go around — it just can't be stopped, started, or broken.
